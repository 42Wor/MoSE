MoSE: Lower Bounds of Expert Granularity in Sparse Transformers on Resource-Constrained Hardware

Maaz Waheed

Independent Researcher

Email: mday2492@gmail.com

Abstract

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures increase model capacity through conditional computation, but conventional expert configurations can impose substantial memory and computational requirements on resource-constrained hardware. This paper investigates Mixture of Smaller Experts (MoSE), a sparse causal Transformer designed to explore practical expert granularity under a restricted GPU memory budget. MoSE uses four Transformer blocks, 16 routed micro-experts per layer, Top-2 token routing, and an unrouted persistent shared expert. The model contains 5,938,816 parameters, with 3,168,384 active parameters per forward pass. Training is evaluated on a mobile NVIDIA Quadro T1000 GPU with 4 GB GDDR6 memory using automatic mixed precision and memory-mapped token streams. The reported peak training VRAM is 860 MB, and sustained local throughput is 5,200–5,600 tokens/s. Across 3,400 optimization steps, validation perplexity decreases from an initial random baseline of approximately 9,934 to 77.38. Qualitative fill-in-the-middle samples show progression from incoherent fragments to structured Python expressions. Router observations indicate persistent token-structural specialization, including loop-associated behavior in Expert 13 and frame-exit behavior in Expert 6. These results establish an empirically viable micro-expert operating point; they do not constitute a universal theoretical lower bound.

Index Terms—Mixture of Experts, sparse Transformers, conditional computation, expert granularity, edge training, memory-efficient language modeling, fill-in-the-middle.

I. INTRODUCTION

Large language models achieve capacity through parameterized transformations, deep architectures, and extensive training. Dense Transformer feed-forward layers execute their principal transformations for every token, coupling model capacity to compute and memory costs. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures partially decouple these quantities by routing tokens to a selected subset of expert networks [1], [2].

Large-scale sparse systems such as Mixtral 8×7B demonstrate the utility of conditional computation, but coarse expert dimensions and total parameter storage remain challenging for many local or edge devices [3]. Fine-grained expert segmentation, as explored by DeepSeekMoE, provides a complementary strategy: divide expert capacity into smaller units and preserve shared computation for common patterns [4].

This work studies a compact operating point using micro-experts with intermediate dimension 128. The goal is to characterize feasibility and observed behavior on constrained hardware, not to claim a proven universal minimum. The title’s “lower bounds” therefore refers to empirical granularity exploration; a formal lower bound on expert dimensions or achievable quality remains open.

Contributions

• A four-block sparse causal Transformer with 16 routed micro-experts per layer, Top-2 routing, and one persistent shared expert.

• An observed 860 MB peak VRAM profile on a 4 GB NVIDIA Quadro T1000, using FP16 AMP and memory-mapped token streams.

• A convergence record reaching reported validation perplexity 77.38 at Step 3400.

• Qualitative FIM progression and routing observations of persistent token-structural expert associations.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Sparse Mixture of Experts

Shazeer et al. introduced sparsely gated MoE layers, using learned gates to selectively activate a subset of feed-forward experts [1]. Fedus et al. proposed Switch Transformers, simplifying routing and developing load-balancing objectives for large sparse models [2]. MoSE follows conditional computation while reducing expert intermediate dimensions and targeting local GPU execution.

B. Fine-Grained Experts and Shared Computation

DeepSeekMoE studies fine-grained expert segmentation and shared-expert isolation to encourage specialization while retaining common computational pathways [4]. MoSE adopts these motivations at a smaller scale: 16 routed experts with dimension 128 and one unrouted shared expert in each block. The present experiment does not include an ablation that proves the shared expert is necessary.

C. Fill-in-the-Middle

Fill-in-the-Middle (FIM) trains autoregressive models to generate a missing span conditioned on surrounding prefix and suffix text [5]. We use a fixed Python scaffold as a qualitative probe. The samples are illustrative and do not establish code execution correctness.

III. THE MOSE ARCHITECTURE

A. Backbone and Micro-Expert Formulation

MoSE is a causal decoder-only Transformer with four blocks, model dimension d_model = 192, six attention heads, head dimension d_head = 32, and sequence length 256. Each block has N = 16 routed experts with intermediate dimension d_expert = 128 and one persistent shared expert with d_shared = 128.

Eᵢ(x) = Wᵢ,₂ φ(Wᵢ,₁ x + bᵢ,₁) + bᵢ,₂

Wᵢ,₁ ∈ ℝ¹²⁸ˣ¹⁹² ,   Wᵢ,₂ ∈ ℝ¹⁹²ˣ¹²⁸                        (1)

Each expert maps a 192-dimensional token state through a 128-dimensional intermediate representation and back to 192 dimensions. Total parameters are 5,938,816; reported active parameters per forward pass are 3,168,384, and the reported sparsity ratio is 46.65%.

B. Top-2 Gating

zₜ⁽ˡ⁾ = W_g⁽ˡ⁾ xₜ⁽ˡ⁾ ,   W_g⁽ˡ⁾ ∈ ℝ¹⁶ˣ¹⁹²

pₜ,ᵢ⁽ˡ⁾ = exp(zₜ,ᵢ⁽ˡ⁾) / Σⱼ₌₁¹⁶ exp(zₜ,ⱼ⁽ˡ⁾)                        (2)

The gate selects the two largest probabilities: Tₜ⁽ˡ⁾ = Top-2(pₜ⁽ˡ⁾). Selected weights are normalized over the chosen set, gₜ,ᵢ = pₜ,ᵢ / Σⱼ∈Tₜ pₜ,ⱼ, and the routed output is R⁽ˡ⁾(xₜ) = Σᵢ∈Tₜ⁽ˡ⁾ gₜ,ᵢ⁽ˡ⁾ Eᵢ⁽ˡ⁾(xₜ).

C. Persistent Shared Expert

S⁽ˡ⁾(x) = Wₛ,₂⁽ˡ⁾ φ(Wₛ,₁⁽ˡ⁾ x + bₛ,₁⁽ˡ⁾) + bₛ,₂⁽ˡ⁾

F⁽ˡ⁾(xₜ) = R⁽ˡ⁾(xₜ) + S⁽ˡ⁾(xₜ)                        (3)

The shared expert is active for every token independent of router decisions. It is motivated as a common computational anchor alongside specialized routed paths. This semantic-anchor interpretation is a design rationale, not a causal conclusion; shared-expert ablation is required to test necessity.

D. Switch-Style Load Balancing

fᵢ = (1/T) Σₜ 1[i ∈ Tₜ] ,   Pᵢ = (1/T) Σₜ pₜ,ᵢ

L_aux = α N Σᵢ fᵢ Pᵢ ,   α = 0.01 ,   N = 16                        (4)

L = L_task + L_aux

The reported auxiliary loss stabilizes near 1.02–1.04. The objective encourages global load balance but does not require identical token-conditioned specialization.

IV. SYSTEM AND HARDWARE OPTIMIZATION

A. Hardware and Precision

The target is a mobile NVIDIA Quadro T1000 Laptop GPU (Turing architecture, 4.00 GB GDDR6 VRAM). Training uses FP16 automatic mixed precision through torch.amp. The reported peak training VRAM is 860 MB, approximately 21.5% of nominal 4 GB capacity.

B. Memory-Mapped Data Engine

Training and validation tokens are stored in flat continuous one-dimensional numpy.uint16 binary streams and accessed with np.memmap. train.bin contains 28,177,334 tokens (53.7 MB); val.bin contains 1,584,516 tokens (3.0 MB). The mapping avoids

materializing the full corpus as a conventional host array and accesses file-backed pages. This is not literal zero physical RAM usage: operating-system page cache, I/O, and host-to-device transfers remain.

The reported task and validation losses coincide at Steps 600, 1000, and 3400. These equalities may reflect rounding or measurement conventions; the supplied record does not establish the exact evaluation aggregation.

C. Routing Specialization and Entropy

Normalized entropy is H_norm(p) = −Σᵢ pᵢ log(pᵢ)/log(N). The reported normalized entropy falls from 75.0% to 45.3% for return and 47.6% for for. E13 retains dominant association with the reported “###” pattern on for loops at Steps 200, 400, and 1000. return and try are reported to converge on E6, interpreted as frame-exit/control-flow-related specialization. E14 is mentioned in the broader analysis, but no token-frequency matrix or precise assignment data are supplied; no separate quantitative E14 conclusion is drawn.

The auxiliary loss range and entropy observations are compatible with stable specialization alongside load balancing. Stronger evidence would require full routing matrices, token-conditioned frequencies, independent runs, and controlled ablations.

VI. DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS, AND FUTURE WORK

A. Expressivity and Routing Overhead

The number of unordered Top-2 expert pairs among 16 experts is C(16,2) = 120 per layer. Across four layers, 120⁴ = 207,360,000 possible sequences of pair choices. This is a combinatorial count of routing configurations, not distinct learned functions. Routing, dispatch, aggregation, and memory-access overhead may become proportionally more significant as experts shrink; hence smaller experts do not guarantee higher throughput.

B. Limitations

• The model is 5.94 M parameters; conclusions do not directly scale to billion-parameter systems.

• Only one GPU platform and a limited set of checkpoints are reported.

• Learning rate, optimizer specifics, batch size, scheduler, and some measurement conventions are absent from the supplied record.

• FIM evidence is qualitative and based on a fixed Python prompt; it does not establish functional code correctness.

• Routing specialization lacks complete token-to-expert frequency matrices, statistical tests, and independent-run reproducibility.

• No expert-size, Top-k, shared-expert, or auxiliary-loss ablation is provided; 128 is an empirical operating point, not a proven theoretical minimum.

C. Dynamic NVMe-to-VRAM Streaming

A future system could keep expert weights on NVMe and cache selected experts in VRAM using a least-recently-used (LRU) policy. Such a system would require asynchronous transfer, residency tracking, prefetching, cache eviction, synchronization, and—during training—management of gradients and optimizer states. The trade-off depends on expert access frequency, storage bandwidth, transfer latency, and compute time.

VII. CONCLUSION

MoSE explores a fine-grained sparse Transformer operating point for local resource-constrained training. A four-block model with 16 routed micro-experts per layer, Top-2 routing, and a persistent shared expert contains 5,938,816 parameters and reportedly trains with 860 MB peak VRAM on a 4 GB Quadro T1000. Validation perplexity falls to 77.38 by Step 3400, while qualitative FIM samples and routing observations suggest progressive syntax acquisition and token-structural expert associations. The evidence supports feasibility at d_expert = 128, but does not establish a universal lower bound. Controlled expert-dimension ablations, stronger code benchmarks, full routing statistics, and NVMe expert streaming remain important next steps.
